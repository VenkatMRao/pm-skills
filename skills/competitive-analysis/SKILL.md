# Competitive Analysis

Produce a competitive analysis that ends in a recommendation, not just a feature
matrix. A table of checkmarks is easy to build and easy to ignore — the point of
this skill is the "so what" section at the end.

## Process

1. **Establish the frame first.** Before comparing anything, confirm (or infer
   from context and state explicitly):
   - Which competitors actually matter here — direct substitutes the target buyer
     considers, not every company in the category.
   - What decision this analysis is meant to inform (a roadmap bet, a pricing
     change, a positioning pitch). The comparison should be shaped by that decision
     — don't produce a generic landscape when the user needs an answer to a
     specific question.

2. **Gather what's available.** Use whatever the user supplies (competitor sites,
   docs, reviews, call notes, pricing pages) plus your own knowledge. State clearly
   which claims are sourced from provided material vs. general knowledge, and flag
   anything likely stale — competitive facts (pricing, feature sets) go out of date
   fast.

3. **Structure the comparison around the decision, not an exhaustive feature list.**
   Pick the 5-8 dimensions that actually matter to the decision from step 1 (e.g.
   pricing model, core workflow, integration depth, target segment) rather than
   every feature every product has.

4. **Write the analysis:**
   - **Summary** — one paragraph, lead with the answer to the question this
     analysis was meant to inform.
   - **Comparison table** — dimensions as rows, competitors as columns.
   - **Per-competitor notes** — 2-4 sentences each: what they're clearly better at,
     where they're weak, and what that implies for us.
   - **Strategic implications** — concrete: "this suggests we should/shouldn't ___
     because ___." This is the section a feature matrix can't produce on its own,
     and it's the one most worth getting right.
   - **What we don't know** — gaps in the research, named explicitly rather than
     glossed over.

## Guardrails

- Don't overstate confidence in claims about a competitor's internals (roadmap,
  strategy, financials) that aren't publicly verifiable — mark those as inference,
  not fact.
- Don't recommend a course of action the evidence doesn't support just to make the
  analysis feel conclusive. "The evidence here doesn't clearly favor either option"
  is a legitimate finding.
